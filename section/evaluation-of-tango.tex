\section{Evaluation of TANGO}
\label{sec:evaluation-of-tango}

In this section, we first evaluate the SLO attainment and goodput of \tango{} across different models and multi-SLO workloads.
We then investigate the effectiveness of its two scheduling components, validate the step-time model, examine sensitivity to sending rates and SLO settings, evaluate scalability with larger models and GPU deployments.
%  and analyze scheduling overhead.

\subsection{Evaluation Setup}
\label{subsec:evaluation-setup}

Table~\ref{tab:motivation-setup} shows our experimental platform.
The chatbot, mathematical-reasoning, and software-agent workloads are described in Section~\ref{subsec:motivation-trace}.
We test the Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-32B (8B, 14B, and 32B for short).
Based on the parameter size, we use 1, 2, and 4 NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPUs to deploy them, respectively.
Each GPU has 48\,GB of GPU memory.
% Moreover, we use four SLO classes (TTFT-prioritized, TPOT-prioritized, balanced, and relaxed) for each model.
For each trace, we randomly partition its requests into four equal-sized groups and assign each group to one of the four SLO classes (TTFT-prioritized, TPOT-prioritized, balanced, and relaxed) defined in Section~\ref{subsec:motivation-trace}.

We use the popular serving systems vLLM, SOLA, and JITServe as baselines.
We evaluate two vLLM variants: vLLM-Colocated executes prefill and decode on the same GPUs, allowing prefill chunks and decode tokens to share a batch, whereas vLLM-Disaggregated executes the two phases on separate GPU pools.
% The disaggregated variant is evaluated for the 14B and 32B models; it cannot run in the single-GPU configuration used for the 8B model.
vLLM-Colocated employs FCFS admission and a fixed maximum per-step token budget.
%  without differentiating request-specific TTFT and TPOT objectives.
SOLA adjusts both request execution order and iteration workload according to request and system states to improve SLO attainment.
JITServe estimates uncertain request characteristics and allocates serving bandwidth to individual requests, while grouping compatible requests to improve batching efficiency.
Since SOLA's source code is not publicly available, we reimplement SOLA's scheduling mechanisms on top of vLLM 0.17.0.
%and JITServe uses a different vLLM version
% This allows all systems to be compared on the same vLLM runtime.

Following prior works, we use overall SLO attainment and goodput as performance metrics.
Overall SLO attainment is the percentage of all requests that satisfy both their TTFT and TPOT SLOs.
Goodput measures the number of such requests completed per second.

\subsection{SLO Attainment and Goodput}
\label{subsec:slo-attainment-and-goodput}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/evaluation/new_experiment_combined_pd.pdf}
  \caption{The overall SLO attainment and goodput of \tango{}, vLLM-Colocated, vLLM-Disaggregated, SOLA, and JITServe. Purple crosses indicate that vLLM-Disaggregated cannot run in the single-GPU 8B configuration.}
  \label{fig:slo-attainment-and-goodput}
  \vspace{-3mm}
\end{figure}

We first evaluate \tango{} on overall SLO attainment and goodput.
Each workload is evaluated on the 8B, 14B, and 32B models.
For each model--workload combination, we replay the trace at $1.2\lambda_{90}$, where $\lambda_{90}$ is the sending rate at which vLLM-Colocated achieves 90\% SLO attainment.% for that combination
% All systems use the same request sequence, arrival schedule, and SLO targets.
Fig.~\ref{fig:slo-attainment-and-goodput} shows the results for all nine model--workload combinations.

We can first observe that vLLM-Disaggregated achieves substantially lower overall SLO attainment and goodput than \tango{} and even vLLM-Colocated in all cases.
The purple crosses for the 8B model indicate that vLLM-Disaggregated cannot run in this single-GPU configuration.
%  as prefill and decode require separate GPUs.
On average, its SLO attainment is 77.56\% and 66.20\% lower than \tango{} and vLLM-Colocated, respectively, with corresponding relative goodput reductions of 82.80\% and 74.33\%.
Given this extremely poorer performance, 
% we use the stronger vLLM-Colocated variant to represent vLLM in all subsequent experiments.
% Accordingly, 
all improvement figures reported below relative to vLLM use vLLM-Colocated as the baseline, and vLLM denotes this colocated variant throughout the remainder of the evaluation.

Compared to the remaining baselines, \tango{} also achieves the highest overall SLO attainment and goodput in all test cases.
Across the nine cases, the average relative improvements in SLO attainment are 58.72\%, 50.94\%, and 26.64\% compared with vLLM, SOLA, and JITServe, respectively.
The corresponding average goodput ratios are $1.52\times$, $1.47\times$, and $1.22\times$.
For the best case of Qwen3-32B mathematical-reasoning, \tango{} achieves $2.50\times$, $2.44\times$, and $1.20\times$ the goodput of vLLM, SOLA, and JITServe, respectively.

These improvements are consistent with \tango{}'s joint control of request urgency and step duration.
The dispatcher prioritizes earlier deadlines for admission and preempts a later-deadline running request when KV-cache allocation fails.
The allocator uses predicted step time and active latency slack to bound the prefill work sharing a step with decode requests.
This coordination addresses the TTFT violations from queueing and TPOT violations from mixed prefill/decode execution identified in Section~\ref{subsec:motivation-underlying-reasons}.

\subsection{Effectiveness of Temporal Request Dispatcher}
\label{subsec:effectiveness-temporal-scheduling}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/evaluation/ablation_goodput_slo.pdf}
  \caption{The goodput and overall SLO attainment of \tango{}'s variants normalized to \tango{}, represented by bars and curves, respectively.}
  \label{fig:ablation-goodput-slo}
  \vspace{-3mm}
\end{figure}

In this subsection, we show the performance of \tango{}-WOT, a variant of \tango{} that disables the temporal request dispatcher.
It restores vLLM's default admission and preemption policies, including FCFS request admission, while retaining the \allocator{}.
All other experimental conditions are kept the same as in Section~\ref{subsec:slo-attainment-and-goodput}.
%including the nine model--workload combinations and sending rates, 

The green bars and green curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation-goodput-slo} show the goodput and overall SLO attainment of \tango{}-WOT normalized to \tango{}.
As observed, both metrics decrease in all nine cases, with average relative reductions of 20.31\% and 20.39\%, respectively.
Without phase-aware request prioritization, FCFS can leave requests with tight TTFT targets waiting behind earlier requests with more latency slack.
The default preemption policy also does not compare active deadlines when deciding which running request should yield KV-cache capacity.

Although the retained allocator regulates the execution time of mixed batches, it cannot by itself give earlier admission to urgent waiting requests.
Consequently, controlling step duration alone is insufficient to recover the SLO attainment and goodput of the complete system.

\subsection{Effectiveness of Spatial Scheduling}
\label{subsec:effectiveness-spatial-scheduling}

In this subsection, we show the performance of \tango{}-WOS, a variant of \tango{} that disables spatial token allocator.
It replaces model-guided token allocation with a fixed maximum token budget per step, while retaining the \dispatcher{}.
Thus, request admission and preemption still use phase-aware deadlines, and all other experimental conditions remain unchanged.

The blue bars and blue curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation-goodput-slo} show the goodput and overall SLO attainment of \tango{}-WOS normalized to \tango{}.
Both metrics remain below those of \tango{} in every case, with average relative reductions of 13.19\% in goodput and 12.66\% in SLO attainment.


A fixed token budget does not provide a reliable bound on the duration of a mixed prefill/decode step.
As batch composition and context lengths change, the same token count can incur different computation and KV-cache access costs.
A long prefill chunk can thus delay the next tokens of decode requests even when request admission follows deadline order.
The model-guided allocator controls this interference by adjusting the prefill allocation to the active decode workload and remaining latency slack.
These results show that deadline-aware request ordering and execution-time-aware token allocation make complementary contributions to serving performance.

\subsection{Accuracy of the Step-Time Model}
\label{subsec:accuracy-step-time-model}

In this subsection, we evaluate the prediction accuracy of the step-time model in Eq.~\ref{eq:step-time-model}.
For each deployment configuration, we compare the predicted duration $\widehat{L}$ with the measured duration $L$ using its fitted coefficients.
The evaluated steps cover decode-only and mixed execution across the three workloads at low and high loads.
We evaluate 32,542, 30,988, and 21,952 steps for the 8B, 14B, and 32B models, respectively.
We measure prediction error as the absolute percentage error $100\lvert\widehat{L}-L\rvert/L$.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{figures/evaluation/model_accuracy_combined_2x2.pdf}
  \caption{Step-time prediction accuracy for the three models. Diagonal dashed lines denote perfect predictions; color indicates local step counts (log scale). The bottom-right panel shows absolute percentage error CDFs.}
  \label{fig:model-accuracy-combined}
  \vspace{-3mm}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:model-accuracy-combined} shows predicted versus measured step times and absolute percentage error CDFs (bottom right).
In the first three panels, color indicates the number of steps near each plotted location on a logarithmic scale, with purple indicating fewer steps and yellow indicating more.
As observed, predictions closely match measurements across all three models.
The mean absolute percentage errors (MAPEs) are 1.83\%, 1.62\%, and 1.70\% for the 8B, 14B, and 32B models, respectively.
The fractions of steps with errors below 5\% are 90.59\%, 93.70\%, and 91.38\%, respectively.
At 10\%, the corresponding fractions reach 98.68\%, 99.05\%, and 98.36\%.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Sending Rate}
\label{subsec:sensitivity-sending-rate}

In this subsection, we investigate the performance of \tango{} under different sending rates.
The overall comparison in Section~\ref{subsec:slo-attainment-and-goodput} uses $1.2\lambda_{90}$ to evaluate serving performance under pressure.
Here, we vary the load multiplier $\alpha$ from 1.0 to 1.4 in increments of 0.05 and replay the trace at $\alpha\lambda_{90}$.
We use Qwen3-14B with the chatbot workload for this experiment, and other scenairos show similar results.
All systems use the same request sequence, arrival schedule, and SLO targets at each sending rate.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/evaluation/multi_qps_goodput_slo.pdf}
  \caption{The goodput and overall SLO attainment of \tango{}, vLLM, SOLA, and JITServe under different sending rates for Qwen3-14B on the chatbot workload. The load multiplier is relative to $\lambda_{90}$.}
  \label{fig:multi-qps-goodput-slo}
  \vspace{-3mm}
\end{figure}


Fig.~\ref{fig:multi-qps-goodput-slo} shows the goodput and overall SLO attainment of \tango{} and the baselines.
At $\lambda_{90}$, without additional stress, all four systems achieve high SLO attainment and similar goodput, with \tango{} still leading in both metrics.
As the sending rate increases, both metrics generally decline, but \tango{} maintains higher SLO attainment and goodput, showing a more pronounced advantage under pressure.

These results are consistent with \tango{}'s coordinated control of request ordering and step duration.
As the sending rate increases, more requests compete for execution, making queueing delay and prefill/decode interference harder to control.
The dispatcher prioritizes urgent requests for admission, while the allocator adjusts the prefill work in each step according to the predicted duration and active latency slack.
This coordination helps \tango{} preserve higher SLO attainment and goodput under increasing pressure, although both metrics also decline at higher loads.
% The results show that \tango{}'s performance benefits persist across the evaluated sending rates.

\subsection{Sensitivity to SLO Settings}
\label{subsec:sensitivity-slo-settings}

In this subsection, we evaluate \tango{} under different SLO settings.
For Qwen3-14B on the chatbot workload, we scale both TTFT and TPOT targets by $\eta\in\{0.8,0.9,1.1,1.2\}$.
We also combine requests from different trace types into a mixed workload.
All requests of the same trace type share one fixed TTFT/TPOT SLO configuration, while different types can use different configurations.
All systems use identical requests and arrivals within each setting.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/evaluation/multi_trace_goodput_slo.pdf}
  \caption{The overall SLO attainment and goodput of \tango{}, vLLM, SOLA, and JITServe under different SLO settings. }
  \label{fig:multi-trace-goodput-slo}
  \vspace{-3mm}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:multi-trace-goodput-slo} shows that \tango{} achieves the highest overall SLO attainment and goodput in all five settings.
Across the four SLO scaling factors, the average relative improvements in SLO attainment are 38.23\%, 35.16\%, and 27.38\% compared with vLLM, SOLA, and JITServe, respectively.
The corresponding average goodput ratios are $1.29\times$, $1.28\times$, and $1.16\times$.

For the mixed trace, \tango{} increases SLO attainment by 120.27\%, 165.04\%, and 32.25\% over vLLM, SOLA, and JITServe, respectively, with corresponding goodput ratios of $2.02\times$, $2.47\times$, and $1.19\times$.
These results show that deadline-aware request ordering and step-time-guided token allocation remain effective under both SLO scaling and mixed workloads.

\subsection{Scalability with Larger Models and More GPUs}
\label{subsec:scalability-larger-models}

In this subsection, we investigate the scalability of \tango{} with larger models and increased GPU resources.
We use Llama-3.1-70B and Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct (70B and 80B-A3B for short), with the latter using a mixture-of-experts (MoE) architecture.
Both models are deployed on eight NVIDIA A800 GPUs with a tensor-parallel degree of 8.
We evaluate them on the chatbot, mathematical-reasoning, and software-agent workloads, using the same sending-rate and SLO settings as in Section~\ref{subsec:slo-attainment-and-goodput}.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/evaluation/more_model_combined.pdf}
  \caption{The overall SLO attainment and goodput of \tango{}, vLLM, SOLA, and JITServe with the 70B and 80B-A3B models on the three workloads.}
  \label{fig:more-model-combined}
  \vspace{-3mm}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:more-model-combined} shows the overall SLO attainment and goodput of \tango{} and the baselines.
As observed, \tango{} achieves the highest values for both metrics in all six model--workload combinations.
The average relative improvements in SLO attainment are 45.07\%, 33.92\%, and 41.49\% compared with vLLM, SOLA, and JITServe, respectively.
The corresponding average goodput ratios are $1.40\times$, $1.35\times$, and $1.40\times$.
The above results demonstrate that \tango{} remains effective when scaling to larger dense and MoE models, as well as deployments with more GPUs and greater aggregate GPU memory.

\subsection{Overhead of TANGO}
\label{subsec:overhead-of-tango}

\textbf{Time Overhead:}
It mainly comes from deadline maintenance, request ordering, and preemption decisions in the dispatcher, and decode-deadline selection, step-time prediction, and prefill-budget search in the allocator.
Both components run on the CPU before model execution and reuse vLLM's existing executor.
The closed-form step-time model requires only a few arithmetic operations for each prediction.
For a scheduling step with thousands of candidate requests, the estimated CPU time for deadline maintenance and decode-deadline selection is less than 0.5\,ms in total, while step-time prediction and budget search take less than 0.1\,ms.
Since model execution typically takes tens to hundreds of milliseconds per step, this estimated overhead is usually below 1\% of the step latency.

\textbf{Memory Overhead:}
\tango{} stores additional scheduling state as CPU-side metadata, including request SLOs, arrival and latest-token timestamps, generated-token counts, effective deadlines, and progress information.
A compact representation is estimated to require approximately 96 bytes of persistent metadata per request, corresponding to about 240\,KB for 2,500 resident requests.
Moreover, the allocator stores the fitted step-time coefficients and a few configuration values, with a total footprint below 128 bytes.
Its temporary decode-deadline list requires about 16 bytes per active decode request.
The per-request state scales linearly with the request count and is negligible compared with the memory occupied by model weights and KV caches.
